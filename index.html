About

I'm Andrew Orkney, a postdoctoral researcher at Cornell University, College of Veterinary Medicine, Department of Biomedical & Translational Sciences. I formerly worked with the Hedrick Lab studying the evolution of morphological traits in birds and bats, and I now work with the Das Lab where I am cultivating new skills in computational genetic methods. I am seeking faculty positions which present an opportunity to explore tantalizing questions in evolutionary biology, mentor and lecture students. Accessible summaries of my ongoing research questions are presented in my 'Projects' tab, and I will provide research and teaching statements upon request.

My essential interests in science are animals and mathematics; researching biological evolution proivdes a jungle-gym in which they coincide. I am motivated by an essential desire to explore. My research projects are primarily concerned with the process of research and the different possible approaches and paths that exist towards solving problems. My past research has been focused on animal skeletons, using sophisticated high-resolution CT-scanning and digital reconstruction techniques to build quantitative datasets of animal traits, which are then investigated in linear algebraic frameworks. More recently, I have become increasingly interested in data embedding approaches, techniques to describe full manifold geometry of biological surfaces (including soft tissues such as brains), and approaches in evolutionary modeling that may encompass 'structural missingness', so that diverse datasets with sparse trait information can be investigated. All this provides an excellent opportunity to involve students in engaging state-of-the-art research while also teaching them valuable transferable skills in maths and computing (in particular approaches in R and Python) that will benefit them in a wide variety of future career paths. My most recent work now encompasses computational genetics, wherein I am applying sparse matrix decomposition methods to produce quantiative heuristics that describe the properties of highly repetitive non-coding regions, whose sequence properties are hypothesized to encode evidence of evolutionary changes in mechanisms that orchestrate chromosome segregation and recombination. Beyond data collection and analysis, I am committed to closely mentoring students to develop their public presentation skills and confidence, reflected by my receipt of a Cornell University Postdoctoral achievement award for excellent in mentoring. I view mentoring as one of the most enjoyable aspects of my work and a major avenue to improve participation and joy among historically under-represented groups of people in science.

Outside of work I am a trained artist. I paint animals, participate in and help to set up exhibitions. See my 'Art' tab for examples of artwork which are included in my published and upcoming manuscripts. As a new faculty hire I will therefore bring not only excellence in research and a commitment to teaching that includes student participation in publications, but also a capacity to contribute to University cultural activities, especially those which encourage public engagement with the biological sciences.

The Big Question

My research projects all surround the same Big Question. Much the evolutionary sciences is concerned with the what and the why; what adaptations does an organism possess that enable it to survive, and why does it have them? I am more interested in the 'why not'. Why do some organisms not acquire adaptations that might otherwise benefit them? This meta-level question seeks to understand the processes which regulate adaptive response and we can imagine that they are contingent on initial conditions, stepping-functions that determine how integenerational variety is produced, and stochastic variables.

Bats

I study bats because they are an amazingly diverse collection of animals, doing all sorts of jobs. Many use sophisticated echolocation to hunt insects, but others might seek-out delicious fruits, drink nectar, blood, catch fish, lizards or even eat other bats. As part of this work I compiled a large dataset of 111 CT scans of bat skeletal features and undertook an extensive literature review to build design matrices of ecological properties that permit the exploration of adaptation. Now, bats may be exceptionally diverse, but major changes in adaptation are actually quite rare, being limited to certain parts of the wider bat family tree. There are entire bat families whose ecologies are actually rather uniform, and whose environmental distributions are limited, often being restricted to tropical centeres of diversification. Therefore, bats are an excellent model system in which to explore the variables that permit adaptive differentiation; what are the stars that must align to permit shifts in ecological function and the morphological and behavioral traits that facilitate it? The two main areas I am set to explore are bat brain evolution and the intersection between sexually differentiated traits and changes in sex chromosomal system.

Sexual dimorphism; is chromosomal rearrangement a gas-pedal for evolutionary innovation?

Sexual difference: A dataset being compiled by Post-Baccalaureate Caroline Goldstein suggests sexual difference in bat pelvis shape is so salient that it is evident even across multiple species separated by tens of millions of years of evolutionary divergence.

Female bats give birth to young that may weigh as much as a third of their body mass, meaning that the female and male bat pelvis bones experience very different selective forces and may have distinct optimal shapes (sexually antagonistic selection). Indeed, the bat pelvis bone is an incredibly sexually dimorphic structure. The relocation of coding genes that control the development of sexually dimorphic traits on to the sex chromosomes is one potential means by which sexually antagonistic selection can be accommodated by evolving animals. Ongoing work is exploring the evolution of 3D pelvis shapes across bats, novel sex-determining chromosomal systems and the resolution or persistence of sexual antagonism. Together, these insights proffer a deeper insight into sexual dimorphism- the chasmic dichotomy at the heart of what it means to be a sexually reproducing animal.

Surprisingly, while bats exhibit extreme sexual dimorphism in their pelvis shapes, there is scarce evidence that bird pelvis (known in birds as the 'synsacrum') shapes differ between males and females- even though the female pelvis must accommodate egg-laying and therefore has a different suite of functional challenges to the male pelvis. Perhaps, though, the extreme task of laying eggs in teeny-tiny hummingbirds, which can weigh as little as 2 grams, will prove an exception! The female hummingbird (the hummer-mamma) may even lay an egg that weighs more than her entire skeleton. Surely then, the hummer-mamma and hummer-papa will possess different pelvic shapes...and if they do not, then surely this indicates some amazingly restrictive and strong limiting obstacle in bird evolution.

Our research in bats will complement and inform research into sexual differences in pelvis shape in humans, especially in light of recent publications refuting a trade-off between locomotory and reproductive functions in women. Further analyses will reveal whether functional trade-offs exist across bat evolution, whether they are sex-specific, and help further refine thinking about the evolutionary context of sexual difference in other animals such as humans.

Hummingbird happenings

I recently demonstrated that the strength of correlated evolutionary changes between bird wing bone proportions is much more intense in heavy birds than tiny species. This might happen because the high stresses of carrying a heavy body requires bones to work together to resist strain, forcing them to evolve in unison. This may mean that small bird species, which are not encumbered by this restriction, can explore novel wing proportions and designs more freely as they evolve- potentially explaining why small bodied bird species that are relatively closely related can exhibit a surprising diversity of flight styles. For example, swifts, nightjars and hummingbirds (all members of 'Strisores' or the 'Nightbirds') may soar, bat to and fro erratically, or undertake spectacular hovers.

Mass matters: as body mass increases (x-axis) the degree of independent evolution (y-axis) between wing bones (humerus and carpometacarpus) gets smaller and smaller. This may be caused by increasingly restrictive mechanical demands upon the skeleton. A.Orkney Nat.Comm. 2024

I am currently seeking funding to go further and specifically explore whether wing bone evolution differs between teeny tiny and super-massive hummingbird species, which differ in mass by a factor of 10! Prospective students will help design and place landmarks on high-resolution CT-scans of hummingbird skeletons to quantify the evolution of their wing bone proportions. I am also cultivating interest in sexual dimorphism in the hummingbird pelvis; see the 'Sexual dimorphism' project!

Animal asymmetry

Vogue research questions surrounding asymmetric features in normally bilaterally symmetric vertebrate characteristics have repeatedly suggested that reductions in population size and or genetic diversity can increase developmental instability- manifesting in asymmetry. Asymmetry is therefore increasingly treated as a proxy of population level fitness and a herald of population viability, with major conservation implications. However, asymmetry might also foster deep insights into the evolutionary processes that produce novel radiations of animals in the first place. Researchers have recurrently suggested that weakened developmental stability in isolated populations may facilitate adaptive selection. Perhaps flirting with extinction places species in the crucible that produces macroevolutionary change. Large scale efforts to investigate indices of asymmetry across diverse vertebrate radiations are required to address this question. This future research will intersect with efforts to study whether exotic karyotypes in bats are associated with or have facilitated phenotypic novelty and how normally pathological phenotypes might be re-contextualized and re-purposed towards adaptive functions, such as cleft palate, changes in phalangeal formula, membranous webs, tooth reduction and more!
